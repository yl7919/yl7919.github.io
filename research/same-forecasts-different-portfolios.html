Same Forecasts, Different Portfolios: Factor Normalization and Regularization

Why two versions of one factor model with the same forecasts can hold different portfolios when a penalty on factor exposures is written in each version’s own units, and what using the model’s estimated intercept adds.

Working paper, October 2026 · 116 pages: a 46-page main text and a 70-page Internet Appendix (cited as pages IA-1 to IA-70) · not yet posted to SSRN, no DOI · page numbers refer to the paper above

Two versions of one factor model can forecast the same stock returns and still hold different portfolios. The portfolio rule adds a small constant (a ridge; the paper calls it the factor penalty) to each factor’s variance. That constant penalises how much the portfolio bets on each factor, in the units each version happens to use, so one number is two different penalties (eq. 6, p. 11). Over 594 months, the two versions’ stock forecasts differ by a median of 4.8 parts in a million (Table II, p. 24). Yet at the same penalty number their Sharpe ratios (average excess return per unit of volatility) before trading costs are 0.69 and 1.07 (Table I, p. 19). Written in one set of units, the penalty gives one portfolio; without it, both versions are at 1.62.

A portfolio needs a return forecast as well as a risk penalty, and the second result turns to the forecast. The same kind of model can also estimate an intercept, the part of expected return that none of its factors explains. Using the full fitted mean, intercept included, gives a Sharpe ratio of 2.51 before trading costs that barely moves with the penalty. Its gain over the best portfolio without an intercept is 0.89, with a 95% interval of [0.59, 1.26], and 0.57 [0.29, 0.89] net of 25 basis points per dollar traded (Table III, p. 30). Since October 2007 the net advantage has an interval that includes zero (pp. 7, 39).

1 The question

In the agenda, this paper asks: same forecast, different portfolio? Interpreting Pricing Errors asks what a model’s alpha means; this paper moves from reading the model to acting on it. The Ruler Matters then holds the target fixed and asks: same target, different stocks?

If two versions of one factor model make the same forecasts but measure factor exposure with different rulers, do they tell the investor to hold the same stocks? And what does using the intercept the model already estimates add?

To say how much of a stock’s expected return each driver explains, a model must first decide when two combinations of characteristics are really different, and how large each one is. The rule it uses for that is the ruler: technically, a metric (an inner product) on characteristic space. Give each characteristic a weight and add them up: every stock gets a score. Such a weighted combination is called a direction (it is not a portfolio). The plain ruler (the Euclidean or identity metric) judges a direction by its weights alone. The characteristic ruler (the Gram metric, built from the Gram matrix of the characteristics: their average products across stocks) judges it by the scores it gives real stocks: how large they typically are, and whether two directions’ scores line up across stocks, high on the same stocks and low on the same stocks. So the ruler is the convention a model uses to measure how large a direction is and how much two directions overlap. Two value signals that pick out the same cheap stocks count as overlapping under the characteristic ruler but as unrelated under the plain ruler.

The paper compares two separately estimated versions of one factor model that differ only in the ruler. IPCA (instrumented principal component analysis) is a standard conditional factor model in which characteristics determine each stock’s factor loadings, that is, how strongly the stock moves with the common drivers (Kelly et al. 2019). Its hidden (latent) factors have no units of their own, so a model must fix them; the paper calls this choice the normalisation. Fixing them with the plain ruler gives the Euclidean normalisation, IPCA’s usual restriction; fixing them with the characteristic ruler, the metric of Characteristic-Space Metrics (Liu 2026b), gives the Gram normalisation (eqs. 2–3 and footnote 3, p. 10). A third fit adds an intercept: the part of expected return that no factor in this model explains (the home page’s “alpha” is a close relative, measured against a stated benchmark model). It is the NA-IPCA (No-Arbitrage IPCA) of Geometric Framework (Liu 2026a), whose “no-arbitrage” is a cap on how much the intercept may carry; the cap never binds in this paper (footnote 3, p. 10).

2 Why it matters

Factor models are compared by the Sharpe ratios of their portfolios, but a portfolio is a forecast passed through a risk matrix, and that matrix carries settings written in the model’s own units. If a setting moves holdings, a manager who switches or re-estimates a risk model may read the new positions as a new signal when nothing in the forecast has changed.

  • Economic. The same penalty number decides a comparison across model families. At the reference penalty, risk-premium PCA (RP-PCA, a competing way of extracting factors) has a Sharpe ratio before trading costs of 1.49 against 0.69 for IPCA with the plain ruler. Without the penalty, the gap of 0.80 becomes −0.08, with an interval of [−0.44, 0.26] (Table I, p. 19; Table II, p. 24).
  • Practical. A constant added to factor variances for numerical stability is a penalty on factor exposures. Written in one set of units, or directly on stock positions, it cannot move the portfolio when the normalisation changes (Proposition 1, p. 12). The paper’s design rule: state the penalty, the units it is written in, and which part of the fitted mean the portfolio uses (p. 43).
  • Statistical. The reference penalty is about 11 times the median factor variance in plain-ruler units and 27 times in characteristic-ruler units. The factor covariances are invertible without it (smallest eigenvalue 3.1 × 10⁻⁵), so it is not needed for that purpose (Internet Appendix IA.E.2, p. IA-22).

4 The framework

The portfolio rule holds more of a factor when its forecast is high relative to its estimated risk. Before inverting the factor risk, it adds a small constant (0.01, a ridge; the paper calls it the factor penalty) to each factor’s variance, which makes the rule hold a little less of every factor. That constant charges the portfolio’s exposure to each factor in the units in which the factor loadings are reported, so changing the ruler changes how strongly each stock position is held back (eq. 6, p. 11).

Changing the ruler is a matched change of units: every fitted return and residual stays the same, the loadings are rescaled, and overlap moves among the hidden factors, the observed Fama–French factors and the intercept (Proposition 1, p. 12). Of the portfolio rule’s inputs, only the penalty changes: doubling a factor and halving its loadings turns the same penalty into a quarter of itself on that factor (pp. 11, 13). Proposition 1 gives the map between the two versions and shows that their penalised portfolios coincide if and only if the penalty is written in one set of units: chosen under one ruler and carried to the other, or set directly on stock positions. Neither choice says what the penalty should be, because the fit is the same under every normalisation and cannot select it (Proposition 1, p. 12; p. 13).

The intercept enters through the expected-return input. The penalty acts on a portfolio only through its factor exposures. A portfolio built from the factor means alone carries its whole forecast through those exposures; adding the intercept adds positions whose expected return the factor block does not carry, so the penalty barely reaches them (Lemma 1, pp. 14–15).

Technical details

The model writes stock i’s next-month excess return as (eq. 1, p. 9)

r_{i,s+1} = z_{i,s}'\Gamma_\beta f_{s+1} + z_{i,s}'\Gamma_\delta g_{s+1} + z_{i,s}'\Gamma_\alpha + \varepsilon_{i,s+1}.

Here z_{i,s} lists the 132 characteristics, f the three hidden factors and g the five Fama–French factors; each \Gamma turns characteristics into loadings, and IPCA sets \Gamma_\alpha = 0. A normalisation with metric Q imposes \Gamma_\beta' Q \Gamma_\beta = I_K and \Gamma_\beta' Q [\Gamma_\delta, \Gamma_\alpha] = 0: the plain ruler sets Q = I_L, the characteristic ruler an average of the window’s Gram matrices W_s = Z_s'Z_s/N_s (eqs. 2–3, p. 10). The intercept fit also bounds the average squared stock-level intercept by 0.035; the bound is slack at every date, with a largest value of 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ across the 618 rolling fits (footnote 3, p. 10).

With B_t the stocks’ factor exposures, \hat\mu_t the forecast and A_{0,t} the risk matrix without the factor penalty (it keeps a position penalty \lambda_\Sigma = 0.01 written directly on stock positions), the portfolio solves (eq. 6, p. 11)

\max_w \; w'\hat\mu_t \;-\; \tfrac12\, w'A_{0,t}\, w \;-\; \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\lVert B_t'w\rVert^2 .

A change of normalisation maps B \to BJ^{-1}. Adjustments R and R^Q added to the two factor covariances give the same stock risk matrix, and the same portfolio for every expected-return vector, if and only if R^Q = JRJ'; for R = R^Q = \lambda I with \lambda > 0 this holds if and only if J is orthogonal, which the map from the plain to the characteristic ruler is only when the two metrics already agree on the hidden loadings (Proposition 1, p. 12; p. 13). For any expected-return vector, the penalty shrinks the portfolio’s factor exposures as B'w_\lambda = (I+\lambda M)^{-1}B'w_0 with M = B'A_0^{-1}B, and without the penalty the intercept portfolio is the same under every normalisation (Lemma 1, pp. 14–15).

How to read it. Stock 1 loads on factor 1 (mean 0.01, variance 0.02, loading 1); stock 2 loads on an independent factor whose units never change. Each stock has residual variance 0.01 plus its own penalty of 0.01 on stock positions. Converting the ridge with the units is the paper’s remedy, which it calls writing the penalty in one set of units; removing the penalty is the paper’s other benchmark (Proposition 1, p. 12; Table IA.A.1, p. IA-4). Source: illustrative arithmetic on the portfolio rule in the paper (eq. 6, p. 11); no data files.

Two cooks follow the same recipe. One measures flour in cups, the other in half-cups. The recipe also says “add a pinch of salt per unit of flour”, so the half-cup cook adds more salt, although the flour is the same. The difference lies in how the rule is written, not in the ingredients. There are two fixes: drop the rule, or convert it with the units. In the paper, the flour is the forecast, the unit is set by the ruler, and the pinch is the penalty of 0.01 added to each factor’s variance. Unlike salt, the penalty is a charge that makes the rule hold less of each factor; it grows with the square of the exposure, and a real change of ruler also mixes exposures.

6 Design and main results

The data are monthly U.S. stock returns and 132 characteristics from Jensen et al. (2023), for mega-, large- and small-capitalisation stocks (microcaps excluded), ranked each month (Section III.A, p. 16). Each month every model is refitted on the previous 120 months and its portfolio is held for one month, over 594 formation months from June 1975 to November 2024 (pp. 16–17). Each IPCA fit has three hidden factors and the five Fama–French factors as observed factors (Fama and French 2015). One mean–variance rule sets every model’s weights on individual stocks, and positions are scaled each month to a total size of one, long plus short; the paper calls this unit gross exposure (pp. 17–18). The factor penalty takes six values from 0 to 0.1, with 0.01 as the reference (p. 17). Intervals are pointwise 95% intervals from a paired moving-block bootstrap with 12-month blocks and 10,000 draws that holds the fitted models fixed (Section III.D, p. 20). Sharpe ratios are annualised and before trading costs unless stated. Costs are quoted in basis points (bp; 1 bp = 0.01 percentage point) per dollar traded.

1. Same forecasts, different holdings. If the units did not matter, the two versions would hold the same stocks. They do not: at the reference penalty, written in each version’s own units, one minus the similarity of their weights has a median of 0.17, and the typical distance between their weights is more than half of the total position (p. 21; Table II, p. 24).

Weight similarity is the cosine of the two weight vectors: 1 minus it is 0 for identical proportions. The distance is the sum of absolute differences between the two portfolios’ weights, each scaled to a total position of one, so it runs from 0 to 2. The forecast gap is the size of the forecast difference relative to the plain-ruler forecast (Table II, Panel A, p. 24).

The two versions are estimated separately, so this agreement is measured, not built in. Written in one set of units, the reference penalty gives one portfolio, and the returns follow the weights: the characteristic-ruler fit with its penalty written in plain-ruler units has the plain-ruler fit’s Sharpe ratio, 0.69 (p. 22; Table I, p. 19). The holdings gap therefore comes from writing the penalty in two sets of units.

7 How it relates to prior work

One literature estimates factor models from many characteristics; another regularises risk estimates for portfolio choice. This paper studies the step between, where a factor model’s forecast and risk become a portfolio.

Prior work What it established What this paper adds
Kelly et al. (2019) Characteristics instrument time-varying factor loadings (IPCA); a zero intercept is not rejected once several hidden factors are included The normalisation IPCA leaves free sets the units of any factor-covariance penalty; the intercept is used as a portfolio input, a different question from their test (Section V.C, p. 31)

A Sharpe ratio of 2.5 sits beside Kelly, Pruitt and Su’s finding that this model’s intercept cannot be distinguished from zero, and the two answer different questions. Their test asks whether the intercept loadings, one per characteristic, are jointly large; the portfolio asks whether the intercept summed across some two thousand stocks, weighted by the inverse risk matrix, has a mean per unit of risk. The second can be large when the first is not (Section V.C, p. 31).

Geometric Framework and Characteristic-Space Metrics show that the ruler decides how a fit is split without changing the fit. This paper takes its characteristic ruler from Characteristic-Space Metrics and its intercept fit, NA-IPCA, from Geometric Framework (footnote 3, p. 10), and follows a change of the ruler’s units into a portfolio: it matters only through a penalty written in those units. The Ruler Matters measures the same ruler on the stocks, and asks which familiar positions a model’s factors represent and what hedging them requires.

Interpreting Pricing Errors and Same Forecasts, Different Portfolios both ask what the model’s leftover is worth, but in different settings. The first shrinks one characteristic-based model’s alpha component (keeps an estimated fraction of it) to make forecasts more accurate under squared-error loss. The second adds the NA-IPCA intercept to expected returns inside one fitted model and measures a portfolio Sharpe ratio before trading costs. The models, objectives and samples differ, so neither result confirms or contradicts the other.

The tools are known. What is new is a check and a measurement: whether holdings move when only the units of a fixed factor penalty change, with the map that removes the dependence, and how using the full fitted mean changes a portfolio’s sensitivity to the same penalty (p. 8). It is a check, not a new estimator.

The results are conditional evidence on model-based allocation, not a verified implementable trading strategy: a stock enters a month only when its next-month return is recorded, and the point-in-time availability of the characteristics is not independently verified (p. 7; footnote 7, p. 16).
  • Pointwise intervals. Intervals hold the rolling fits fixed and are not joint across penalties or portfolios. The 594-month sample and the plain intercept portfolio as the main-text rule were chosen after both samples and both rules were computed, and the intervals do not adjust for that (footnote 2, p. 6; footnote 12, p. 20). On all 618 months, the corresponding difference between the rulers, 0.27, has an interval that includes zero (footnote 1, p. 4; Table IA.E.6, p. IA-29).
  • One model, not model families. Proposition 1 maps two normalisations of one model; it says how to carry a penalty across them, not what its strength should be, and it does not equate model families, whose forecasts differ (pp. 4, 13).
  • The intercept is not shown to be mispricing. It is the part of the fitted mean that the factor block does not absorb; whether it reflects pricing errors, omitted factors or conditional dynamics is not imposed, and its alpha is a residual relative to the stated regressors (pp. 5–7; footnote 18, p. 27).
  • No best ruler or penalty. The ranking of the two rulers is a function of the penalty, and the fit cannot select the penalty (pp. 13, 23). In this sample no shrinkage of the factor covariance is needed; the design rule that does not depend on the sample is to write any penalty in stock units (p. 26).
