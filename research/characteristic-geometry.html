Feed both to the same portfolio rule and the holdings differ: the gap between the two sets of positions is about 60 percent of the size of the positions themselves (Table V). 1 The puzzle: same forecasts, different portfolios

Take two factor models built from the same 132 firm characteristics and the same 618 months of U.S. stock returns (Section II.A). A factor is a common source of return that many stocks share; a characteristic is a measurable trait of a company, such as its size or its book-to-market ratio. Both models use three statistical factors and the five Fama-French factors, five published benchmark factors. Their next-month return forecasts agree to about five parts in a million in a typical month: the median relative difference is 4.7 parts per million (Section III.B). Hand both forecasts to the same portfolio rule with the same settings. The two portfolios are visibly different books. The median cosine between their weight vectors is 0.83, where 1 means the same direction, and in half the months it lies between 0.75 and 0.88 (Section III.B). The forecasts never come close to that: the largest relative gap between them in any month is 140 parts per million (Section III.B). The median relative gap between the raw weight vectors is 0.607 (Table V).

Nothing in the forecasts explains this. The paper asks what does. The answer turns on a quantity most research never mentions: the ruler. A factor model must decide what one unit of exposure means and when two exposures count as unrelated. IPCA, the standard model, uses a plain ruler on coefficient vectors, as if each of the 132 characteristics were an independent, equally scaled coordinate. QZ-IPCA uses a ruler built from the data, the characteristic Gram matrix, which records how much the 132 characteristics overlap across stocks (Section I.B). The two models differ only in that ruler.

So the research question is this. Once a model has produced its forecast, how does characteristic geometry, meaning the scale and overlap of firm characteristics, change the portfolio an investor actually holds? (Abstract; Introduction.)

Here is the mechanism in two sentences. The portfolio rule adds a small fixed safety penalty, 0.01, to every factor’s risk, and it adds it in each model’s own units. The two rulers give the factors different units, so the same 0.01 is a different penalty, and the rule responds to the penalty, not to the forecast (Section IV.B). Sections 5 and 7 show this in arithmetic and in the data. Section 8 adds a second finding the paper meets on the way.

2 Why it matters: three reasons, each with a number

Because a forecast is not a portfolio, and almost all attention goes to forecasts. Every quantitative equity strategy has two steps. First, predict which stocks will do better. Second, turn the prediction into positions. The second step passes through a risk model, and a risk model has units. This paper shows that the units are part of the strategy.

First, the puzzle above is measurable. Two investors with identical forecasts can hold different books for reasons that have nothing to do with information. Comparing forecasts alone would miss this source of variation in holdings (Introduction).

Second, a signal is left out. Factor models estimate an intercept: the part of expected return that characteristics predict but the factors do not explain. A portfolio built from the factor terms alone leaves it out (Section V.A). Within one fitted NA-IPCA model, switching that intercept off gives a gross Sharpe ratio of 0.39; putting it back into expected returns gives 2.51 over 594 months (Table VII, Panel A). Gross means before all trading, financing and borrowing costs, and the page repeats that wherever the number appears. One more thing shapes how to read it. How much of a stock’s expected return lands in the intercept rather than in the factor terms is set by the ruler, not by the data alone (Section I.D). So the number is the value of using one block of one decomposition, and Section 8 says what that block contains.

Third, the characteristic library is crowded. Across 618 months, the 132 characteristics behave like about 20: a statistic the paper calls the participation dimension, roughly the number of equal-sized directions it would take to carry the same spread of exposure, has a median of 19.8 (Figure 1; Table IA.III). Most new signals re-describe old exposures. When most signals point at the same few exposures, the rule for measuring exposure decides how much of a forecast becomes a bet.

The durable lesson is smaller than any Sharpe ratio and more useful. Before you read a change in holdings as new information or a new risk, check the units and the regularisation (Section IV.C).

3 How the paper answers it: change one thing at a time

The design is a controlled comparison. Almost everything is held fixed and one thing changes at a time (Table II).

Data. 132 stock characteristics from the Jensen et al. (2023) panel for U.S. mega, large and small caps, micro-caps excluded. Each characteristic is rank-scaled every month: converted to its percentile rank among all stocks and shifted to run from minus 0.5 to plus 0.5 (Section II.A). Every month the model is refitted on the previous 120 months and used to build a portfolio for the next month only. That gives 618 months in which a portfolio is built, from June 1973 to November 2024, with about 2,000 stocks a month (Section II.A). A stricter 594-month sample from June 1975 is used whenever a rule needs 24 prior estimates of its loadings (Section II.B).

Models. Three specifications share three latent factors and five observed Fama-French factors and differ only in the ruler and the intercept (Table I). IPCA: plain ruler, no intercept. QZ-IPCA: Gram ruler, no intercept. NA-IPCA: Gram ruler plus a fitted intercept, kept below a size cap that in practice is never reached (Table I; Section II.A). Because the cap is never reached, the estimated intercept is the same one an uncapped fit would produce. In this sample the cap names the specification and gives the intercept a unit of measurement; it does not change any estimate (Appendix IA.1.3). The paper’s “no-arbitrage” label is therefore a statement of motivation, not of a restriction that shaped the results. The Gram ruler is built three ways inside each window: EWMA (an average that weights recent months more, with a 60-month half-life), an equal-weight 120-month average, and a rolling 60-month mean (Section II.B). All three are reported; none is selected by significance. Two benchmarks run under the identical protocol: FF5 (the five Fama-French factors alone) and RP-PCA (a variant of principal components that also fits average returns, Lettau and Pelger 2020). An archived PCA (plain principal components) configuration is excluded because its forecast is degenerate, not as evidence against PCA (Section II.C; Appendix IA.2.2).

Portfolio rule. One mean-variance rule for every model: the rule bets more where expected return is high and risk is low, so weights equal the inverse of a risk matrix times expected returns (Section I.F, equations 8 and 9). To keep the arithmetic stable it adds a small constant, 0.01, to every factor’s risk and to every stock’s risk; the paper calls this a ridge, and this page calls it the safety constant or the ridge, nothing else. That constant is the hinge of Sections 5 and 7. Each month the positions are scaled so that long plus short exposure equals one dollar (equation 10), so comparisons are about direction, not leverage. Performance is gross of costs; a cost proxy appears only as a sensitivity check (Section II.C).

Inference. A paired moving-block bootstrap: the same 12-month blocks of history are resampled for both strategies, 10,000 times, with 24- and 60-month blocks as stress checks (Section II.D). Three things a careful reader should know. Pointwise means each interval is a 95 percent statement about its own comparison; across the dozens of comparisons on this page, the chance that at least one excludes zero by luck is well above 5 percent, and no correction for that is applied. The bootstrap resamples realised monthly returns with the fitted models held fixed, so it measures sampling variation in the performance comparison, not uncertainty in the estimated loadings, the ruler, or the choices of ridge, window and factor count (Section II.D). A Sharpe difference is the difference of the two strategies’ Sharpe ratios recomputed in each resample, not the Sharpe ratio of the return difference, and mean tests recentre the paired difference at zero before resampling (Appendix IA.5.3).

4 Where it sits: IPCA, the ruler, and a bounded intercept

IPCA (Kelly et al. 2019) writes each stock’s next-month excess return as its characteristics times a coefficient matrix, in three blocks: three latent factors the model discovers, five observed Fama-French factors, and an intercept that does not move with any factor (Section I.A, equation 1). Loadings, the sizes of a stock’s bets on each factor, are not fixed numbers; they are weighted sums of the stock’s 132 characteristics, so they move when the characteristics move. The data pin down only the sum of the three blocks, which the paper calls the complete fitted index. How that sum is split is a convention, and the ruler sets it.

IPCA measures a loading direction the way you would measure a list of numbers: square each entry and add. It treats each of the 132 characteristics as its own axis, all the same size and all at right angles. QZ-IPCA measures the same direction by the size of the bet it creates across stocks: a direction’s squared length is the average squared exposure it generates in the cross-section, computed from the Gram matrix, the average across stocks of products of characteristics (Section I.B, equation 3). Under that ruler two directions count as unrelated only if the stocks they pick do not overlap. Several value measures and several momentum measures pick many of the same stocks, so the two rulers disagree. Two loading vectors can be perpendicular on paper and still buy the same stocks.

Proposition 1 (Section I.D) is the hinge. Switching ruler is a matched transformation. The latent loadings are re-based to unit length under the new ruler. Whatever part of the Fama-French loadings and the intercept overlaps with the latent directions moves into the latent block. The latent factor series is rescaled and shifted so that it carries exactly what moved (equation 6). Every fitted return and every residual comes out identical. Only the labels and the units change. Like quoting a salary in euros a month instead of dollars a year: every number on the page changes, and nothing about the job does. The general construction and its inference theory are in a companion paper, Characteristic-space metrics in factor models: identification and inference (Liu 2026); this page states only the case the portfolio analysis needs (Section I.D; Section I.E).

NA-IPCA adds one piece: a characteristic-driven intercept, kept orthogonal to the latent block under the ruler and capped by an exposure budget (Section I.C, equation 5; budget 0.035, Table I). The name refers to bounding departures from a zero-intercept pricing benchmark. It is not a claim that the intercept is a pricing error, and reading it as mispricing would need a specified pricing benchmark (Section I.C). Two more cautions. The intercept is orthogonal to the latent loadings in characteristic space, but the positions it adds to a portfolio can still carry factor exposure (Section V.A, equation 18), so its return is not a zero-beta return and not alpha after factors. And the budget caps the intercept’s contribution relative to how much characteristic exposure a portfolio takes, not relative to its risk, so it is not a bound on a Sharpe ratio or on distance from a pricing kernel (Appendix IA.4.1). The budget is the one place where a ruler change can exclude a model rather than relabel it (Section I.D); in this sample it never binds, so that possibility is not observed.

The ridge is the bridge to portfolios. The portfolio rule maximises expected return minus one half of estimated variance (Section I.F). The ridge adds a charge to that objective: one half of 0.01 times the sum of squared factor exposures, plus the same charge on squared stock positions (Section IV.B, equation 14). One way to read it: adding 0.01 to each factor variance treats every factor as riskier than its sample history by the same fixed amount in its own units. A charge written in units that change with the ruler is a different charge under each ruler. The stock-position charge is already ruler-proof, because a position in a stock means the same thing under any ruler; that is why the fix is to write the factor charge the same way. Everything else in the objective survives the change of ruler. The next section shows it in arithmetic.

5 A simple example in two parts

Two small calculations, using only the paper’s algebra (Sections I.B and IV.B). No data are needed. The numbers are illustrative units, not estimates from the paper.

Part 1: what the Gram ruler sees. Suppose there were only two characteristics, book-to-market and earnings yield. Scale each so that its average squared value across stocks is 1, and suppose their average product across stocks is 0.9: most stocks that are cheap on one are cheap on the other. The Gram matrix records those averages: 1 and 1 on the diagonal, 0.9 off it (equation 3). The paper’s rank-scaled characteristics have average squared value near one twelfth, which only changes the units. The Gram matrix records average products, not correlations: it is a second moment and equals a covariance only after centring (Section I.B).

Now take two loading directions. Direction a scores a stock on both cheapness measures added together. Direction b scores one minus the other. Under the plain ruler both have the same length and sit at right angles. Under the Gram ruler the average squared score across stocks is, for a, (1 + 0.9 + 0.9 + 1) / 2 = 1.9, and for b, (1 minus 0.9 minus 0.9 + 1) / 2 = 0.1.

A stock that is cheap on both measures gets a big score from a and almost none from b. So a is a real bet and b is nearly nothing, even though the plain ruler calls them the same size. The Gram ruler sees that the two cheapness measures mostly buy the same stocks; the plain ruler does not. With 132 characteristics the paper finds that the eigenvalue mass of the ruler is spread over the equivalent of about 20 equal directions, a measure of overlap rather than a count of independent bets (Figure 1, Panel A; Section III.A).

Direction a (both measures added): Gram length² = (1 + 0.9 + 0.9 + 1) / 2 = 1.9. Direction b (one minus the other): Gram length² = (1 − 0.9 − 0.9 + 1) / 2 = 0.1. Under the plain ruler both have length² = 1 (Section I.B, equation 3).

The bridge from overlap to units. Switching to the Gram ruler re-bases each direction to length 1 under that ruler. Direction a, whose Gram length is the square root of 1.9, about 1.38, has its loadings divided by 1.38 and its factor multiplied by 1.38. Direction b, whose Gram length is the square root of 0.1, about 0.32, has its loadings multiplied by about 3.2. That is the change of units in Part 2, with k = 1.38 for a and k = 0.32 for b. A fixed ridge of 0.01 now charges the crowded direction about half as much (1 / 1.9) and the spread direction ten times as much (1 / 0.1). This is arithmetic, not a claim about which ruler is better.

Part 2: what a fixed ridge does when units change. Two stocks, two independent factors, each stock loading on one factor. Factor 1: monthly mean 0.01, variance 0.02. Stock 1’s loading on it is 1. Stock 1’s own variance is 0.01. The ridge is 0.01 on the factor and 0.01 on the stock (Section I.F).

Stock 1, original units. Forecast: loading times factor mean, 1 × 0.01 = 0.01. Factor risk: loading squared times variance, 1 × 0.02 = 0.02. Ridge charge: ridge times loading squared, 0.01 × 1 = 0.01. Total risk entry: 0.02 + 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.01 = 0.05. Raw weight: forecast over risk, 0.01 / 0.05 = 0.20.

Now change units. Double the factor: mean 0.02, variance 0.08. Halve the loading: 0.5. Nothing about the stock or the market has changed. This is the same model in different units, which is exactly what a change of ruler does (Section IV.B, equation 12).

Stock 1, doubled units. Forecast: 0.5 × 0.02 = 0.01, unchanged. Factor risk: 0.25 × 0.08 = 0.02, unchanged. Ridge charge: 0.01 × 0.25 = 0.0025, one quarter of before (Section IV.B, equations 13 and 14). Total risk entry: 0.0425. Raw weight: 0.01 / 0.0425 = 0.235.

With one stock this would only rescale the position, and unit-gross scaling removes the rescaling. That is why the second stock is there. Stock 2 is identical to stock 1 but loads on factor 2, whose units did not change. Its weight stays 0.20 while stock 1’s rose to 0.235, so the rule now holds relatively more of stock 1: after scaling to one dollar, the split moves from 50/50 to about 54/46. That relative shift is what survives unit-gross scaling and what Table V measures at full scale. Rescale factor 1 by 10 instead of 2 and its ridge charge falls to one hundredth. A ruler change rescales the latent factor coordinates all at once and mixes the observed-factor overlap into them (the five observed factors keep their units), and it stretches as well as rotates, so the penalty on each affected direction changes (equation 13).

Why does a charge this small move real portfolios? In any direction, the ridge matters in proportion to 0.01 divided by that factor’s variance in its reported units. Here that ratio is one half. Where a factor’s reported variance is small, the ridge is a large share of its risk, and a change of units moves it a lot. The paper does not report which factors sit where; Table V reports the net effect on the weights, and it is large.

The fix takes one line. Convert the ridge with the units: the transported ridge is 0.01 × 2² = 0.04, and 0.04 × 0.25 = 0.01 again, so stock 1’s weight returns to 0.20 (Section IV.B, closing paragraph). 7 What the paper finds, part 2: a fixed ridge in changed units is the culprit

Section IV does the detective work in four steps.

Step one: split the gap exactly. The difference between two mean-variance portfolios can be written, with no approximation, as one piece from different expected-return inputs and one piece from different risk matrices (equation 11). The mean piece is small next to the covariance piece, which points at the risk matrix (Table V). The page does not quote a percentage for the mean channel because the two term norms are not additive shares (Table V note; Section IV.A).

Step two: find the one term that is not invariant. Under a matched change of factor units, the forecast and the unregularised stock covariance are exactly preserved (equation 12). The one term that is not preserved is the fixed ridge of 0.01 on each factor variance. A general change of factor basis does not preserve it; only an orthogonal change would, and a change of ruler need not be orthogonal (equation 13).

Step three: delete only that term. Equation 12 holds by algebra for matched representations. The two models here were estimated separately, so nothing forces it to hold. That is what makes this step a test rather than a tautology: if the two fits were not the same model in different units, deleting the ridge would not make their weights agree. The paper removes the factor ridge from the stored risk matrices, holds forecasts, residual variances and the stock-level ridge fixed, and re-solves the rule in every one of the 618 months (Section IV.C). The median relative weight gap between IPCA and QZ-IPCA falls from 60.7 percent to about 4 in a million. The maximum falls from 89.5 percent to about 0.01 percent (Table V). One minus the weight cosine falls from about 0.17 at the median to about 6 in a trillion, and sits near that floor in every month, recession or expansion (Figure 2). That they agree to a few parts in a million in every one of 618 months is the evidence that the fits are matched and that the ridge was the only input that was not.

Step four: check the plumbing. Rebuilding the factor covariances from the saved factor histories reproduces the stored matrices to within 2 × 10⁻¹⁵ across all 1,236 model-window matrices (Table V). The same pattern appears when both models are started from a common initialisation (Table IA.I), which supports the ridge channel rather than a starting-value explanation.

This is a mechanism check. It shows that the ridge, held fixed while the factor units change, is why the weights differ. It does not compute the returns of ridge-free or transported-ridge portfolios, so it cannot say which convention would have earned more (Section IV.C). The constructive answer is to transport the ridge with the coordinates, or to specify the penalty in stock space (Section IV.B). Before reading a portfolio difference as new information or a new risk, check the units and the regularisation.

9 How this compares with prior work

In plain words, the contribution is two things: a check that any portfolio rule can be put through, does the rule impose the same stock-level penalty after an equivalent change of factor coordinates, and a measurement of one input’s worth inside one fitted model. It is a representation check and a same-fit ablation, not a new estimator.

The forecasting question is in good hands. Kelly et al. (2019) let characteristics drive time-varying factor loadings. Gu et al. (2020) showed that flexible machine-learning forecasts from many characteristics add investor value. Kelly et al. (2024) showed that models with more parameters than observations can forecast better, even with little shrinkage. Lettau and Pelger (2020) showed how to extract factors that respect expected returns rather than only variance. Kozak et al. (2020) showed how to shrink a stochastic discount factor built from many characteristic portfolios toward its high-variance principal components. Jensen et al. (2023) supplied the open data this paper uses.

This paper sits one step later, where a forecast becomes a portfolio. Jagannathan and Ma (2003) showed that a position constraint and covariance shrinkage, pulling noisy risk estimates toward a simpler target, are two faces of the same adjustment, so a constraint and a regulariser are two descriptions of one preference over positions. Ledoit and Wolf (2017) showed how to choose the shrinkage to improve out-of-sample portfolios. Together with DeMiguel et al. (2009) and Kan and Zhou (2007) on estimation risk, that literature works in stock space (Introduction). This paper adds a third description: in a factor model, a regulariser written in factor units is a preference that changes when the units do, so a normalisation choice that is harmless for fit enters the portfolio through the ridge (Section IV.B). In the author’s words, the contribution is to connect characteristic-based return models to explicit choices in portfolio construction, so that the evidence separates differences in fitted models from differences in their use (Introduction).

On the intercept, Kelly et al. (2019) allow a characteristic-driven intercept and test whether it is zero, and Zaffaroni (2025) shows what asymptotic no-arbitrage implies for pricing errors in conditional models (Section I.C). Principal Portfolios (Kelly, Malamud, and Pedersen 2023) separates zero-beta from factor-exposed predictability; the retained intercept here asks a related but different question, what mean information remains after the declared factor blocks, and its portfolio value is not a zero-beta or arbitrage result.

Three works the draft does not yet cite are the closest neighbours. Universal Portfolio Shrinkage (Kelly, Malamud, Pourmohammadi, et al. 2023) designs the shrinkage that maximises out-of-sample performance; this paper offers a representation check that any shrinkage rule could be put through, has not tested that method, and does not claim it fails the check. Machine Learning and the Implementable Efficient Frontier (Jensen et al. 2026) shows that net-of-cost performance needs a cost-aware objective; this paper works upstream of costs and would be a natural input to such an objective. Principal Portfolios is the third, as above.

Three further contrasts help place the work. DeMiguel et al. (2020) show that trading costs change which characteristics matter; this paper shows that the risk penalty changes which positions are taken, for a fixed set of characteristics. Brandt et al. (2009) never form a factor covariance, so the factor-ridge channel studied here has nowhere to enter; that is a strength of their design. Didisheim et al. (2024) find that more factors help; this paper holds the factor count fixed and varies only the coordinates.

The contribution is narrow, and stated as such. A representation result (Proposition 1), stated here as a special case of the companion paper on characteristic-space metrics (Liu 2026), which carries the identification and inference theory. An algebraic observation that a normalisation choice enters the objective through an otherwise fixed ridge, with a two-line remedy (Section IV.B). A stored-matrix experiment that confirms the channel month by month (Table V; Figure 2). A same-fit ablation that isolates the investment value of the retained mean (Table VII). And an explicit list of what is not established.

10 Why practitioners should care

Risk-model conventions are usually treated as housekeeping. This paper says they are part of the strategy. Five situations, then a checklist.

Same forecasts, different books. Two desks license the same forecasts but run risk models that measure exposure in different units. Their optimisers disagree, most of all in the directions whose units differ most between the two risk models (Section IV.B). That is a units disagreement, not a forecasting disagreement. The remedy is to transport the ridge with the coordinates, or to specify the penalty in stock space (Section IV.B; Conclusion).

Migration backtests. A fund switches risk vendors and reruns its history. Month by month, identical forecasts now map to different weights, and turnover between the old and new books is a cost with no forecasting content. The paper’s mechanism check says where such a gap can come from; it does not say which book earns more (Section IV.C).

Crowding. In August 2007 funds with different forecasts held nearly the same positions and unwound them together. Institutional Investor’s ten-year retrospective and its 2024 profile of AQR’s three quant crises both describe that episode as crowding. The paper’s participation dimension measures overlap among characteristic exposures inside one model’s library (Figure 1; Table IA.III). That is a different object from crowding across funds, but it is a price-free complement to order-flow measures such as Risk.net’s 2020 study of factor crowding.

The factor zoo. The “factor zoo” is the hundreds of published return predictors. Institutional Investor reported in 2019 that managers keep launching proprietary factors nobody outside has replicated, and in 2020 that “pure”, non-overlapping factors matter more than adding signals. Overlap is exactly what the Gram ruler records (Section I.B). Before concluding that 30 new alternative-data signals are useless because the model’s fit barely improves, ask how much of their exposure overlaps with the roughly 20 directions already covered. Overlap is what the Gram ruler records; it does not say how many priced risks there are (Section III.A). That is the same scepticism practitioners voiced about big data in 2017 and about AI stock picks in 2026.

Backtest to live. Institutional Investor’s 2018 piece on momentum funds is a reminder that turnover eats paper Sharpe ratios. Every number on this page is gross. Figure 1’s 0, 10 and 50 basis point views exist for that reason, and those lines are a proxy rather than a net return (Section VI.D). An asset owner comparing two backtests should hold the cost convention fixed, just as the paper holds the risk-model units fixed.

Checklist: before you attribute a change in holdings to new information.

  1. Confirm the factor units. Has the loading normalisation or the risk-model metric changed?
  2. Confirm the ridge was transported with the coordinates, or written directly in stock space (Section IV.B).
  3. Compare forecasts and weights separately. Agreement in one does not imply agreement in the other (Section III.B).
  4. Hold the cost convention fixed across the backtests you compare (Section II.C).

11 What this paper does not claim

A page like this earns trust by saying where the evidence stops. The paper is careful about this, and the page repeats every limit.

  1. Gross is not net. All Sharpe ratios and mean increments are before trading, financing and borrowing costs, under a unit-gross leverage policy (Section II.C; Section VI.D). A gross Sharpe of 2.5 is a statement about the information in the fitted mean, not about a strategy you could run tomorrow. A cost charge does not only lower the mean. Turnover moves with returns, so charging for it also changes volatility, and a net Sharpe ratio cannot be read off by subtracting a fee from the gross mean (Appendix IA.4.4, equation IA17). The proxy’s error has no known sign.
  2. The cost proxy is not an implementable return. The archived cost figure charges a proportional fee on raw turnover divided by contemporaneous gross exposure. It gives a cost threshold under the proxy, about 136 basis points for the complete rule and 142 for RP-PCA (Figure IA.2), that the paper does not treat as a break-even cost, and proxy-adjusted Sharpe ratios, 1.62 for the complete rule and 0.27 for IPCA at 50 basis points, labelled as proxies (Appendix IA.4.4). It gives no capacity estimate and no implementable net Sharpe. Figure 1’s cost lines are a sensitivity check only.
  3. Ridge removal is a mechanism check, not a performance test. It shows why the two models’ weights differ. Ridge-free and transported-ridge return paths were never evaluated, so the sign and size of the ridge’s effect on realised Sharpe ratios are not established (Section IV.C; Table V; Figure 2).
  4. Overlap is not a count of priced risks. The participation dimension and spectral drift measure how concentrated characteristic exposures are. Neither estimates the number of independent or priced risks (Section III.A; Table IA.III).
  5. One decomposition ratio is deliberately not quoted. Table V reports the norm of the mean channel relative to the total weight gap. This page does not quote it as a percentage, because the two terms need not be orthogonal and the norm ratios are diagnostic magnitudes, not additive shares (Table V note; Section IV.A). The page says only that the mean channel is small relative to the covariance channel.
  6. The intercept is not shown to be mispricing, a zero-beta result or arbitrage. Its usefulness as a portfolio input is measured. The positions it adds can carry factor exposure (Section V.A, equation 18), its budget bounds exposure rather than risk (Appendix IA.4.1), and a pricing interpretation would need a specified pricing benchmark that the paper does not impose (Section I.C).
  7. No universally optimal ruler is established. The three ruler constructions are all reported and none is selected by significance (Section II.B). All three update over time, so the comparison does not isolate the value of updating itself; a frozen-ruler control is future work.
  8. The ruler comparisons are statistically fragile. On 618 months the EWMA and equal-120 Sharpe intervals include zero, and the rolling-60 interval includes zero at 24- and 60-month blocks (Table IV; Table IA.VI). No mean-return or volatility interval excludes zero. The strict-24 results are retrospective sample-sensitivity checks (Table IA.II).
  9. The intercept effect is concentrated in earlier decades. Block Sharpe ratios are 3.97, 3.03 and 1.13, and no recent-period interval from October 2007 excludes zero (Section VI.C; Table VI). No between-period test is reported.
  10. Intervals are pointwise and retrospective. Each is a statement about its own comparison, with no correction for the number of comparisons, for specification search, or for dependence created by overlapping 120-month estimation windows; the fitted models are held fixed inside the bootstrap (Section II.D). Holm adjustment in Table VI covers the four size universes only.
  11. Table VIII and Table VII are different kinds of comparison. Table VIII compares separately estimated strategies; Table VII is a within-fit input ablation. The intercept-off row is not a separately estimated comparator, and re-estimating a model without an intercept would test something different (Section V.A; Section VI.A).
  12. The instability penalty is a proxy. The instability matrix is built from 24 overlapping estimates, is not a calibrated sampling covariance, and its multiplier of 1 is a specification choice, not an optimum (Section V.B; Table IA.V).
  13. Forecast accuracy is not portfolio value. Table IA.IV evaluates the factor-only NA-IPCA forecast, not the calibration of the complete IAB mean (Section VI.B). The intercept-off forecast has a rank correlation near zero with returns, so 0.39 is not the Sharpe ratio of a good factor model without its intercept.
  14. Data conventions are not fully verified. Full point-in-time availability of inputs, delisting treatment and eligibility conventions remain unverified (Section II.A). One window, June 1999, hits the iteration cap, and the convergence flags do not establish a stationary constrained solution (Appendix IA.1.3).
  15. Unit-gross scaling is a leverage policy, not a validation of raw positions. Before the rescaling, the rule’s raw positions would total about 500 dollars long and short for every dollar of capital (Table IA.VII). The rescaling removes that leverage, which is why every number here is about the direction of the portfolio, not about an amount you could invest. Because the raw scale changes every month, dividing by it is itself a timing decision. The 2.53 belongs to that policy; the same positions at constant leverage would have a different Sharpe ratio (Appendix IA.7.4).
  16. Nothing on this page is investment advice. The numbers describe a research sample, June 1973 to November 2024.

What would settle the open points (the paper’s own future work, Sections II.D, V.B and VI.D): a holdings-based implementation audit with stock-level positions, financing, borrowing fees and capacity; a frozen-metric control with transported risk adjustments, to isolate the investment value of updating the ruler; a direct between-period test of the mean increment; a raw-panel rerun with convergence diagnostics; verification of information dates and delisting treatment; and a pricing interpretation of the retained intercept tied to the SDF benchmark of Section I.C rather than to gross Sharpe.

12 Glossary

Terms in the order a reader meets them. Interpretation limits sit inside the definitions so they cannot be separated from the term.

Factor
A common source of return that many stocks share. The paper uses three latent factors the data discover plus the five observed Fama-French factors (Table I).
Characteristic
A measurable trait of a company known before next month’s return: size, book-to-market, past momentum, profitability and so on. The paper uses 132, each rank-scaled to the interval from minus 0.5 to plus 0.5 every month (Section II.A). One column on a company’s monthly report card.
Sharpe ratio
Annualised average excess return divided by annualised volatility (Section II.C). Return per unit of risk: miles per gallon, not top speed. Every Sharpe ratio on this page is gross of costs.
Gross versus net
Gross means before trading, financing and borrowing costs. The paper’s cost adjustment is a turnover proxy and is explicitly not an implementable net return; a charge changes volatility as well as the mean (Section VI.D; Appendix IA.4.4). A rated fuel economy on a test bench, not the cost of the trip.
Exposure
How much a portfolio’s return moves with a factor or a characteristic; the size of the bet.
Fama-French five factors
Five published benchmark return factors (market, size, value, profitability, investment) used here as the observed factors (Table I).
Ruler (characteristic metric)
The rule that says what unit length and orthogonal mean for loading directions. The plain (Euclidean) ruler measures coefficient length. The Gram ruler measures the average squared exposure a direction actually creates across stocks (Section I.B, equation 3).
IPCA
Instrumented Principal Components Analysis (Kelly et al. 2019). Estimates latent factors while letting each stock’s loadings be a function of its characteristics. Its standard normalisation treats every characteristic as an independent, equally scaled coordinate: the plain ruler (Section I.B).
Gram matrix
A 132-by-132 table, computed each month, recording the average product of each pair of characteristics across stocks (equation 3). It is a second moment, not a covariance, and equals one only after centring (Section I.B). QZ-IPCA uses a trailing average of it as the ruler (Section II.B). It measures overlap, not the number of priced risks. A ruler that knows some roads are the same road under two names.
QZ-IPCA
IPCA with the Gram ruler in place of the plain one, used both to normalise the latent loadings and to separate them from the observed-factor block (Table I). Proposition 1 shows the switch leaves every fitted return unchanged (Section I.D).
Loading
How strongly a stock moves with a factor. Here it is a weighted sum of the stock’s characteristics, so it changes as the characteristics change (Section I.A, equation 1). If a factor is a wind, the loading is the size of the sail.
Ridge (regularisation, safety constant)
A small constant, 0.01 here, added to each factor’s variance and each stock’s variance so the risk matrix can be inverted stably (Section I.F). Written out, the factor ridge is a charge of one half of 0.01 times the squared factor exposures, in the factor’s reported units (equation 14). A safeguard that also states a preference.
Retained intercept (retained mean)
The part of a stock’s expected return that its characteristics predict but the factors do not explain, kept in the model rather than forced to zero (Section I.C, equation 4). Which part of the mean lands here depends on the ruler (Section I.D). Its portfolio value is measured here (Table VII); whether it is mispricing or omitted risk is left open.
NA-IPCA
QZ-IPCA plus a characteristic-driven intercept that is orthogonal to the latent block under the Gram ruler and capped by an exposure budget of 0.035, which never binds in this sample (Section I.C, equation 5; Table I). “No-arbitrage” refers to bounding departures from a zero-intercept benchmark, not to a proof that the intercept is a pricing error.
Mean-variance rule
The portfolio rule used for every model: it maximises expected return minus one half of estimated variance, so weights are proportional to the inverse of the regularised risk matrix times expected returns, then scaled to unit gross exposure (Section I.F, equations 8 to 10).
Unit-gross scaling
Each month the long and short positions are rescaled so their absolute values sum to one dollar (equation 10). A leverage policy that lets strategies be compared on direction, not on borrowing; because the raw scale varies over time, the policy can itself change time-series performance (Appendix IA.7.4).
Rolling out-of-sample window
Each month the model is refitted on the previous 120 months only and judged on the next month; then the window slides forward. No forecast uses its own future. It does not certify point-in-time availability of every input (Section II.A).
Paired moving-block bootstrap
To judge whether one strategy beats another, the same 12-month blocks of history are resampled for both strategies, 10,000 times, with the fitted models held fixed (Section II.D). Pairing keeps both strategies in the same crashes; blocks keep good and bad months clustered. Each interval is pointwise, a statement about its own comparison only. A Sharpe difference is the difference of the two recomputed Sharpe ratios, not the Sharpe ratio of the return difference (Appendix IA.5.3).
Complete fitted index
The total expected return the model assigns a stock, summing the latent, observed and intercept blocks. The data fix the total; the ruler sets how it is split (Section I.A).
Matched representation (Proposition 1)
A change of ruler carried out so that every fitted return and residual stays identical while the loadings, the factor units and the block attribution change (Section I.D, equation 6). Like restating a salary in a different currency. The general result is in the companion paper (Liu 2026).
Transported ridge
The ridge converted along with a change of factor coordinates so that the stock-level penalty is identical under both rulers (Section IV.B). When you change the currency, convert the fee too.
Participation dimension
A number from 1 to 132 summarising how spread out the Gram matrix’s eigenvalues are; median 19.8 across 618 months (Figure 1; Table IA.III). A committee of 132 people with about 20 distinct opinions. It measures overlap among characteristic exposures, not the number of independent or priced risks (Section III.A).
Spectral drift
The month-to-month change, in percentage points, in how the Gram matrix’s eigenvalue mass is distributed across its ranked directions; median 0.063 (Figure 1, Panel B; Table IA.III). It tracks the slice sizes of a pie chart, not whether the slices have been relabelled.
Weight cosine and relative weight gap
The cosine is 1 when two weight vectors point the same way regardless of scale; a median of 0.83 corresponds to an angle of about 34 degrees. The relative gap compares the actual numbers. Both are reported because two portfolios can share a direction and differ in scale (Table V).
Maximum drawdown
The worst peak-to-trough loss along the compounded path of a strategy (Section II.C). A path diagnostic, not an inferential statistic.
Within-fit ablation
Estimating one model, then switching individual portfolio inputs off and on (intercept off, mean-only, complete). Differences come from how the fit is used, not from a different fit (Section V.A; Table VII). Unplugging one component at a time to see which one lights the bulb.
Loading-instability penalty
The complete NA-IPCA rule adds the sample covariance of the last 24 intercept-loading estimates to the risk matrix, charging positions whose intercept signal has wobbled (Section V.B). A numerical proxy, not a calibrated covariance; its multiplier of 1 is a specification choice.
Holm adjustment
A rule that raises the bar for significance when four size universes are tested at once, so one lucky universe is not called significant by chance (Table VI). It does not cover the wider set of specifications tried during research.
Mechanism check
An intervention on stored inputs that identifies why two outputs differ, without measuring what the change would earn. The ridge-removal experiment is one (Section IV.C). A mechanic who finds the rattle has said nothing about whether the car goes faster.
