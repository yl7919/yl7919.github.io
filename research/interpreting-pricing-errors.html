Working paper · September 2026 · 62 pages · not yet posted to SSRN

A new factor model often reports a smaller “alpha”, the part of expected return its factors leave unexplained. Using 153 U.S. stock characteristics (Section 3.1), this paper trains one characteristic-based model with equal and with capitalisation weights, and finds that the large gap between their alphas mainly reflects different estimates of expected returns, not better factors. In this history, residual size adds little to choosing the more accurate forecast, and keeping part of the alpha component improves on the complete forecast but beats simple forecast averaging in only 8 of 24 specifications (Table G.1).

1 The question

When a model reports a smaller estimated pricing error, what has changed, and can the estimate help a user choose or improve a return forecast?

A pricing error, or alpha, is the part of a stock’s expected return that a factor benchmark does not explain (Section 2.1, equation 1). Both the expected return and the factors are estimated, so a reported alpha can shrink because the factors explain more or because the expected return itself changed. The paper separates the two, then asks whether alpha size helps select a forecast and whether the alpha component is useful inside one.

2 Why it matters

Smaller estimated alphas are a common argument for a new model. If the gap mostly reflects a different fitted mean, the argument is about the signal, not the factors.

  • Economic. An alpha is relative to a benchmark, an information set and a horizon. Comparing two estimates without holding the explained signal fixed compares two different objects.
  • Practical. Teams choose between forecasts and decide how much of a model’s alpha component to keep. Both decisions can be tested on later returns.
  • Statistical. An estimated alpha is noisy. Keeping all of it can add error; dropping it can lose signal. Only a matched out-of-sample comparison says which dominates.

4 The framework

For a set of stocks at date t, with expected excess returns \mu_t, factor exposures \beta_t and risk prices \lambda_t, the pricing error is

\alpha_t = \mu_t - \beta_t \lambda_t .

It is in monthly return units (equation 1), and it is not realised noise or proof of mispricing.

The empirical model is a characteristic-based latent-factor model in the style of instrumented principal component analysis (IPCA) (Kelly et al. 2019). Stock characteristics generate both factor exposures, through a loading matrix B, and an intercept (equation 2). The model’s fitted mean coefficient \hat h splits into a factor part p and a characteristic-intercept part r:

\begin{aligned} \hat h &= \underbrace{P_Q(B)\,\hat h}_{p:\ \text{factor part}} + \underbrace{[I - P_Q(B)]\,\hat h}_{r:\ \text{intercept part}},\\ h(\eta) &= p + \eta\, r,\qquad \eta \in [0,1]. \end{aligned}

P_Q(B) projects onto the loading space: every return signal the model’s exposures can produce. Q, a characteristic Gram matrix, measures coefficient differences by how differently they forecast the stocks studied, under equal-stock (EW) or capitalisation (VW) weights (Section 2.2, equations 3 to 5). Under a stated factor-location convention (how the factors’ mean is set), r is exactly the model’s fitted characteristic alpha (Section 2.3, equation 6). When a space is asked to explain another model’s mean, the leftover is a projection residual, not an alpha.

The second half of the display is the forecast-use decision: keep the factor part and a fraction \eta of the intercept part (equation 14). At \eta = 1 the forecast is complete; at \eta = 0 only the factor part is left. Choosing \eta from past forecast errors is what the paper calls residual calibration. Here “residual” means the model’s own intercept part r, not a cross-model projection residual. For the wider characteristic-geometry line, see Characteristic-Space Metrics in Factor Models (Liu 2026) and A Geometric Framework for Identification in Characteristic-Based Factor Models.

6 Design and main results

The data are the U.S. stock panel associated with the characteristic library of Jensen et al. (2023): 153 characteristics and 749 formation months, July 1963 to November 2025. The target is the next month’s excess return, clipped at the monthly 1st and 99th percentiles (Section 3.1). The same model is trained twice on the same stocks: once with equal stock weights (EW) and once with capitalisation weights (VW). Forecasts are issued at nine annual origins, March 2017 to March 2025, for 105 target months, April 2017 to December 2025. Training outcomes always precede forecast outcomes (Section 3.2). Forecast accuracy is prediction R^2 against a zero forecast, as in Gu et al. (2020) (equation 11).

What this shows. Under equal-stock measurement, swapping only the space lowers the residual on the EW mean by 0.70 bp; swapping only the mean lowers it from 117.37 to 75.02 bp (Section 4; Table 1). A symmetric split gives about 101% of the squared gap to the mean and −1% to the space; under capitalisation-weighted measurement, 98% and 2% (Section 4). These are shares, not causal effects; −1% means the space change slightly offsets the mean change. Annual origins give 96.8% to 100.9% (Section 4.1; Table E.4). VW training weights large stocks more and yields a substantially different mean. This locates the gap; it does not say which mean is closer to the truth.

2. Does the gap help select a forecast? At each origin, issue the forecast of the model with the smaller historical residual. Using each model’s own mean, the rule picks the VW-trained model at all nine origins, so it adds nothing beyond “always VW” (Section 5.1). A fairer version judges both spaces against one common target: a ridge regression (a shrunk regression of returns on characteristics) fitted to the same history. Its choices do vary, and Figure 2 tests them.

What this shows. Similar diagnostic advantages precede opposite outcomes: under VW evaluation, advantages for VW of about 2.04 bp (2022 origin) and 1.97 bp (2023) precede a 1.45-point loss and a 1.96-point gain (Section 5.1; Figure 2). Balanced accuracy, the average hit rate in years when EW and when VW turned out better, is 47.5% under EW evaluation and 41.7% under VW, below the 50% that a constant choice would score (Section 5.1). Spanning a common historical target well does not make a model’s own issued mean accurate.

What this shows. Averaging is a demanding control, not a universal winner: under VW evaluation the VW-trained model alone reaches 1.0491%, above the average’s 0.9569% (paper Table 3). Under squared loss, averaging lowers loss below the mean of the two component losses (the expected loss of picking one at random) by a quarter of their squared disagreement, though not necessarily below either component. Here that is worth 0.0700 points of R^2 under EW evaluation and 0.1485 under VW (Section 5.2, equation 13). A selector must earn that back with informative choices. Here the common-ridge selector does not; across the twelve decision sequences it beats the average in five and pooled ridge in four (Section 5.2).

3. Is the component useful inside a forecast? Keep one model and choose how much of its intercept part to keep. The fraction \eta is fitted on the previous twelve completed forecast months, kept in [0, 1], and applied to the next year: eight decisions, 93 target months, April 2018 to December 2025 (Section 6.2).

Figure 3 compares this rule with four baselines: the complete forecast (\eta = 1); the equal average of the EW and VW complete forecasts; characteristic-PC calibration, which splits the same mean with the leading principal components of the characteristics’ second-moment matrix in place of the loading space, then calibrates the remainder; and whole-forecast scaling, which shrinks the entire forecast by one fitted fraction (Section 6.2). What this shows. Scaling the intercept part beats keeping all of it in 20 of 24 specifications, but beats the equal average in only 8 and characteristic-PC calibration in only 6 (Section 6.3; Table G.1). In the primary VW/VW cell it reaches 1.0962%, against 0.9689% for the complete forecast and 0.8876% for the average (paper Table 4). Across K = 1, 3 and 5 in the VW/VW task, its gain over averaging is 0.1513 to 0.2086 points with expanding training but −0.1554 to −0.0739 with rolling training; gains over PC calibration also change sign (Section 6.3; paper Figure 3). So the component can carry useful forecast information, but the gain does not validate the factor-space split, and it depends on the training window.

7 How it relates to prior work

The paper builds on characteristic-based factor models and recent work on their pricing errors, and borrows yardsticks from return prediction and forecast combination.

Prior work What it established What this paper adds
Kelly et al. (2019) Characteristics instrument for factor loadings; an intercept captures characteristic-linked return not due to factor exposure. Uses this setting and asks what a change in the fitted intercept’s size actually reflects.
Zhang (2024) Specification and tests of pricing errors with latent factors and characteristics as covariances. Not a new test; a matched comparison of fitted objects and their forecast use.
Choi and Yuan (2025) Estimation and inference for “inside” and “outside” alphas, with debiasing. A different question: what a reported fall in a fitted alpha changes. The paper does not rank itself against these estimators.
Kim et al. (2021) Attribute characteristic-predicted returns to factor loadings first, and form arbitrage portfolios from the remainder orthogonal to the loadings. Asks a forecasting version: how much of a model’s characteristic-intercept component to keep in an issued return forecast, judged against simple controls and without a portfolio claim.
Kozak et al. (2020) A shrinkage prior on a stochastic discount factor over many characteristic portfolios performs well out of sample; a few principal components summarise it better than a few characteristics. Uses ridge as a prediction benchmark and treats partial retention of the alpha component as a separate, testable shrinkage decision.
Pástor and Stambaugh (2000) Compares pricing models by their investment consequences. Compares them by forecast decisions on a common task, without a portfolio claim.
Bates and Granger (1969) Combined forecasts can beat each component under squared loss. Shows why equal averaging is a demanding control for selection and calibration rules.

The identities that make the objects comparable are shared with the author’s companion work (Liu 2026). The contribution here is empirical: one design, on the same stocks, dates and loss, separates three questions that a residual comparison tends to blur: what explains an estimated alpha’s size, whether the size helps select a forecast, and whether the component helps inside one. The answers are: mostly the mean; little; sometimes. The paper reports the unfavourable cells and the window reversals alongside the favourable ones.

8 Scope

  • Not a pricing-error test. The comparisons do not test whether population alphas are zero (Appendix I.2).
  • Not a trading result. The target is a clipped next-month excess return; prediction R^2 is not a portfolio return, and no costs or constraints are modelled (Appendix I.3).
  • Not independent evidence. Nine annual choices and 24 overlapping specifications are sensitivity summaries, not independent replications or confidence intervals (Section 3.4; Appendix I.2).
  • Not about all alphas. The object is the characteristic intercept under a stated convention, not every stock-level pricing error.
  • Exploratory history. The sample informed the design, and the source return vintage is not fully reconciled (Section 3.2; Appendix I.3).
