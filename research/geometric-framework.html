SSRN preprint, preliminary version, March 2026 · DOI 10.2139/ssrn.7013178 (listed on SSRN under Yang Liu) · Derived from the PhD thesis No-Arbitrage IPCA: A Framework for Cross-Sectional Asset Pricing (available on request). Its identification and inference results continue in the current theory paper, Characteristic-Space Metrics in Factor Models: Identification and Inference.

A factor model built on firm characteristics has to decide which combinations of characteristics are different risks. Most methods use a plain ruler that treats every characteristic as equally scaled and unrelated; this paper takes the ruler from the data, from how characteristics spread and overlap across stocks. In simulations calibrated to U.S. stocks, where the priced directions are the metric’s leading directions, the estimator built on that ruler (NA-IPCA) recovers them almost exactly, while IPCA without an intercept block does not: on a distance scale where 0 means exact recovery, they score 0.01 and 0.73 (paper, Table 8).

1 The question

When firm characteristics are correlated and unevenly scaled, which combinations of them form the priced span of a factor model, and which are only redundant or residual?

Expected returns are now linked to dozens of characteristics at once. Principal component analysis (PCA), instrumented PCA (IPCA) (Kelly et al. 2019) and many machine-learning estimators measure length and orthogonality in characteristic space with the identity matrix (paper, Section 1.1). That choice is seldom stated. The paper makes it explicit and asks what changes when the ruler comes from the data.

2 Why it matters

The ruler decides what counts as “different”. Under the plain ruler, two loading vectors can be perpendicular and still pick the same stocks. An estimator can then split one priced direction into two factors, or let a latent factor copy an observed benchmark factor already in the model (paper, Section 1.1).

  • Economic. Which directions earn a risk premium, and how much pricing is left to the intercept, both depend on the ruler used to tell directions apart (paper, Section 1.1).
  • Practical. A user who reads a latent factor as a new risk may be holding a benchmark exposure twice.
  • Statistical. In-sample fit does not reveal the problem. In the paper’s simulations, IPCA fits returns with an R² between 0.94 and 0.99 even when its latent span is far from the true one (paper, Section 7.3; Table 9).

4 The framework

Let Z_t hold L characteristics for the N_t stocks observed in month t. The paper’s ruler is the long-run characteristic metric (paper, Section 2):

Q_Z = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{1}{N_t} Z_t^{\top} Z_t .

It is the long-run average of the characteristic Gram matrix: how characteristics move together across stocks. Under this ruler the length of a coefficient vector a is (a^{\top} Q_Z a)^{1/2}: roughly, how widely the exposures it creates vary across stocks. Two directions are orthogonal when the characteristic portfolios they create do not co-move in the long run (paper, Section 2).

Expected excess returns are split into three blocks (paper, Section 2):

\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[r_{t+1}\right] = Z_t\left(\Gamma_\beta f_t + \Gamma_\delta F^{\text{obs}}_t + \Gamma_\alpha\right).

\Gamma_\beta loads on latent factors f_t. \Gamma_\delta loads on observed factors, such as traded benchmark factors. \Gamma_\alpha is a characteristic-driven intercept (paper, Table 1). All restrictions are written in Q_Z: the latent loadings are orthonormal (\Gamma_\beta^{\top} Q_Z \Gamma_\beta = I), the observed and intercept blocks are orthogonal to the latent span, and the intercept’s size \Gamma_\alpha^{\top} Q_Z \Gamma_\alpha is capped by a bound \delta. A small \delta approaches strict no-arbitrage (paper, Section 2).

The estimator, No-Arbitrage IPCA (NA-IPCA), is weighted least squares restricted to this set; its solution is characterised by weighted least-squares equations projected onto the tangent space of that set (paper, Proposition 3.1). With a flat ruler and no bound, it reduces to statistical IPCA (paper, Section 8). The paper also derives rotation-invariant asymptotic inference for the identified spans under stated conditions (paper, Section 4).

5 A simple example

Take two characteristics, A and B, say two value measures, with cross-sectional correlation ρ. Under the plain ruler their coefficient vectors are perpendicular, so a Euclidean model can put A in the latent block and B in the benchmark block; under the data’s ruler they overlap by ρ. Let the priced direction load equally on A and B. Drag the slider towards 0 and the two rulers agree; drag it up and the two “separate” blocks together claim more than 100 percent of the priced variance, because the overlap is counted twice. Separating B from A in the data’s ruler gives B only what A leaves unexplained, and the total returns to 100 percent. In U.S. data some pairs of characteristics correlate at 0.94 (paper, Table 5).

6 Design and main results

The paper first estimates Q_Z on the replication panel of Kelly et al. (2019): 37 rank-transformed characteristics including a constant, 12,813 firms and 599 months from July 1964 to May 2014 (paper, Section 6.1; Table 2). It then simulates 1,000 stocks over 240 months with characteristics drawn to match this metric. The design has three latent factors on the leading directions of Q_Z and two deliberately weak observed factors (paper, Section 7.1; Table 7). It compares NA-IPCA, IPCA without an intercept block, and PCA over 300 replications (paper, footnote 11).

Figure 2: The characteristic ruler is far from round. Variance share of the first 15 principal components of Q_Z.

How to read it. Each bar is one principal direction of the characteristic metric. A round ruler would give every one of the 37 directions about 3 percent. Source: SSRN preprint, March 2026, Figure 2, p. 33 (reproduced from the paper). Paper page

What this shows. The first principal component carries 25 percent of characteristic dispersion and the first five carry 62 percent; a flat ruler would give each about 3 percent (paper, Section 6.2; Table 5). Eigenvalues range from 0.002 to 1.0, a condition number of 502 (paper, Table 2). Many raw characteristics are versions of a few traits: size, value, profitability, investment and momentum form tight clusters (paper, Section 6.2). Statistically, a plain ruler gives nearly redundant combinations the same weight as the directions along which firms truly differ. The paper reads these few high-dispersion directions as the ones with the most scope to support risk compensation (paper, Section 1.1); the geometry alone does not show which directions are priced.

Figure 3: When priced directions follow the characteristic metric, NA-IPCA recovers the latent span; IPCA and PCA do not. Distance between estimated and true loading spans, geometry-calibrated simulation.

How to read it. The distance is \lVert \sin\Theta \rVert_F, built from the angles between the estimated and the true span. It is 0 for exact recovery. Its maximum, reached when the estimated span is orthogonal to the true one, is \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73 for the three latent directions and \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41 for the two observed directions (paper, footnote 10). Lower is better. Source: SSRN preprint, March 2026, Figure 4, p. 39, and Table 8, p. 38 (reproduced from the paper). Paper page

What this shows. For the latent span, the distance is 0.011 for NA-IPCA, 0.73 for IPCA and 0.99 for PCA (paper, Table 8). IPCA also lets its latent and observed blocks overlap: 0.069 against 0.0005 for NA-IPCA (paper, Table 8). That is the double counting of the toy example, reproduced in a simulated panel of 37 characteristics calibrated to U.S. data. On the observed block the ranking reverses: IPCA’s distance is 0.23 and NA-IPCA’s is 1.40, close to the maximum of 1.41 for two directions (paper, Table 8). The paper reads this as NA-IPCA declining to price observed factors that were made deliberately weak (paper, Section 7.2).

What this shows. IPCA’s latent distance stays between 0.73 and 0.99 in every geometry, and NA-IPCA’s stays at or below 0.04 (paper, Table 9). In this design the true loadings are the leading eigenvectors of the empirical Q_Z in every row, and only the sampling law of the characteristics changes (paper, Sections 7.1, 7.3). The table therefore shows how sensitive IPCA is to the sampling geometry; it does not test NA-IPCA when the priced directions are unrelated to the metric. NA-IPCA fits less than IPCA, whose R² is 0.94 to 0.99: its R² is 0.58 under the empirical metric, about 0.16 under the identity and flattened laws, and −0.07 under the permuted law (paper, Table 9). In the paper’s correlation sweep the true parameters fit returns almost perfectly, with an R² of 1.00 (paper, Table 10), so NA-IPCA leaves systematic variation unexplained. The paper attributes the gap to NA-IPCA declining to fit directions that violate the geometry (paper, Section 7.3). The statistical lesson cuts both ways: high in-sample fit is not evidence of a correct factor structure, and near-exact span recovery does not guarantee good fit.

7 How it relates to prior work

The paper sits where three literatures meet: large-panel factor models, economic discipline on factors, and shrinkage or machine-learning estimators of the stochastic discount factor (paper, Section 1.2). Each has sharpened what a factor model can identify. The table lists the closest reference points.

Prior work What it established What this paper adds
Approximate factor models (Bai and Ng 2002) How many common factors a large panel has, under weak dependence Once the number is set, asks which directions form the priced span, measured with an estimated metric
IPCA (Kelly et al. 2019) Characteristics instrument time-varying betas; the same model admits a characteristic-driven intercept and observable factors, and tests whether each is zero Writes the normalisation and the separation of the latent, observed and intercept blocks in Q_Z, and caps the intercept; IPCA, with its Euclidean normalisation and no cap, is the flat, unbounded case
RP-PCA (Lettau and Pelger 2020) Adding a pricing-error penalty to PCA gives factors that also fit average returns Places pricing discipline in the feasible set, through the metric and an intercept bound
Weak and spurious factors (Bryzgalova 2015) Factors with weak covariance can look spuriously significant and crowd out true risks Carries the weak-factor concern into characteristic space: argues that a plain ruler lets weak, redundant directions absorb variation, and builds weak observed factors into its simulations
Near-arbitrage bounds (Kozak et al. 2018) Absent near-arbitrage, the SDF is well approximated by a few high-variance principal components Imposes a related bound directly on the intercept block, in the characteristic metric
Shrinking the cross-section (Kozak et al. 2020) A prior that shrinks SDF coefficients on characteristic portfolios, most strongly along their low-variance principal components Uses the characteristic metric not as a penalty but to define the model’s normalisation and the separation of its blocks
Volatility bounds (Hansen and Jagannathan 1991) Asset prices bound the volatility of any valid SDF Motivates a quadratic cap, in the same metric, on the pricing content of the intercept block

The angle is the ruler. Much prior work imposes pricing restrictions on top of a factor extraction whose geometry is already fixed (paper, Section 1.2). This paper makes the characteristic metric an explicit, estimated object. It writes normalisation, block separation and a residual bound in that metric, and gives an estimator and inference for the result. The evidence supports a specific claim, not a general ranking of methods: when characteristics are uneven and correlated, the choice of ruler changes which directions a model identifies. The later paper Characteristic-Space Metrics in Factor Models: Identification and Inference develops the identification and inference part with an estimated metric.

8 Scope

  • The paper does not show that NA-IPCA forecasts returns or builds portfolios better. Its evidence is the geometry of characteristic space and calibrated simulations; a full asset-pricing application is left to a companion study (paper, Section 8).
  • In the simulations the true latent loadings sit on the leading directions of Q_Z (paper, Section 7.1). The results show what goes wrong when a plain ruler meets such a geometry, not that every market has it.
  • The IPCA benchmark in the simulations is the restricted version with no intercept block, while the simulated returns contain one (paper, Sections 7.1-7.2). The comparison is therefore not a test of the ruler alone.
  • Q_Z is an estimated description of one sample, 1964 to 2014. If the distribution of characteristics shifts, it should be re-estimated (paper, Section 8).
  • The asymptotic results hold under stated regularity, identification and centring conditions (paper, Section 4). This page does not present them as a general efficiency result or as local asymptotic normality in Le Cam’s sense.
  • The intercept block is a bounded residual component of the model. It is not a measured mispricing or an alpha.
