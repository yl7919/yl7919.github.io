About

I completed my PhD in Finance at Imperial College London in 2026 and am on the academic job market for the first time this year. I am now a Visiting Researcher at the Imperial Centre of Excellence in Quantitative Finance (opens in a new tab), where I continue the research programme described below.
On the 2026–27 academic job market.

My fields are empirical asset pricing, machine learning and big data in finance, and financial econometrics. My papers form one research programme on a question every characteristic-based factor model must answer before it can say anything: how to measure and compare directions in characteristic space. I call this measurement choice the ruler. It adds no information, yet it can decide which factors a model identifies, how fitted returns are split between factors and alpha, and which portfolio an investor ends up holding. The papers follow the ruler from identification and estimation to portfolio choice; A Geometric Framework develops it into No-Arbitrage IPCA (NA-IPCA), with its identification theory and inference.

Five papers, each isolating one modelling choice:

  1. Same data, different factors. Which ruler should a model use, and what does it identify? A Geometric Framework for Identification derives NA-IPCA, which nests IPCA and caps how much the intercept may carry; in calibrated simulation it recovers the latent pricing span where an intercept-free IPCA benchmark does not.
  2. Same fit, different split. Models with identical fit can credit returns differently to latent factors, observed factors and the intercept. Characteristic-Space Metrics shows that the ruler decides the split and derives inference that carries the ruler’s own estimation error.
  3. Same ruler, different target. When equal- and value-weighted fits of one model report different alphas, is it because their factors explain more, or because the expected-return estimate they must explain (the target) has changed? Interpreting Estimated Pricing Errors shows that the gap mainly reflects the target, not the factor space.
  4. Same forecast, different portfolio. Same Forecasts, Different Portfolios shows that with nearly identical forecasts, two factor normalisations give gross Sharpe ratios of 0.69 and 1.07 under the same numerical penalty, and derives the transformation that aligns the portfolios.
  5. Same target, different stocks. My job market paper, The Ruler Matters, shows that a larger characteristic library can fit returns better while representing familiar momentum and size positions less fully, and that the ruler changes the holdings and risk of portfolios with the same characteristic target.

A project in progress takes this work to corporate bonds, with Prof. Paolo Zaffaroni. Each paper stands on its own; together they show that the ruler is a modelling choice with consequences at every stage, from identification and inference to attribution and portfolio construction.

Research

Job market paper

The Ruler Matters: Factor Exposures, Attribution, and Portfolio Risk

Working paper, October 2026 · 133 pages including the Internet Appendix

Invited seminars: University of Cambridge, Faculty of Economics, 4 November 2026 (scheduled); EPFL, Lausanne, 16 June 2026 (opens in a new tab) (precursor presented as No-Arbitrage Factor Geometry)

  • Paper page
  • Paper (PDF)
  • Slides (PDF) (opens in a new tab)

The question. Firm characteristics are widely used to estimate conditional factor loadings, and a larger characteristic library can fit returns better. A better fit does not show which stock positions the estimated loadings represent. That matters to researchers, who attribute returns to factors, and to investors, who use loadings to control risk. This paper measures which positions they represent.

Contributions
  1. A ruler for factor loadings. The paper compares loading directions by the stocks they select, using the cross-sectional second moment of characteristics (their Gram matrix) as the ruler. The measure is invariant to rotations of the latent factors, so it evaluates the estimated loading space itself rather than an arbitrary choice of factor coordinates.
  2. Better fit, weaker representation. In U.S. equities, with the number of factors fixed, expanding an instrumented principal component analysis (IPCA) model from 36 to 153 characteristics raises in-sample fit, yet the loading space represents momentum and size positions less fully: with an unrestricted intercept, average momentum coverage falls from 82.27% to 62.33%, and larger-firm size coverage from 90.17% to 71.93%.
  3. Fit does not identify attribution. For a larger-firm size position, reallocating exposure shared by observed and latent factors raises the contribution credited to observed factors from 1.38 to 45.53 basis points a month, while their combined fitted contribution stays at 2.16 basis points.
  4. The ruler changes portfolio risk. With coefficients fixed and the same characteristic target, measuring exposure on current rather than annual-origin stocks gives 26.62% to 69.39% lower estimated subsequent return variance in all twelve exploratory positive-target comparisons; nine of the twelve simultaneous 95% intervals exclude zero.

Relative to the literature. IPCA and machine-learning models of returns (Kelly, Pruitt and Su, 2019; Gu, Kelly and Xiu, 2020) are judged mainly by fit; this paper asks what the estimated loadings represent across stocks, and shows that a decline in momentum coverage is an empirical outcome, not mechanical dilution from adding variables. Work on pricing errors with latent factors (Choi and Yuan, 2026) studies alphas; this paper measures how shared exposures move attribution within a fixed fitted return. Work on characteristic portfolios that hedge common risk (Daniel, Mota, Rottke and Santos, 2020) chooses hedges by return covariances; this paper holds the characteristic objective fixed and shows that the exposure reference decides which stocks implement it. Rather than proposing a new estimator, the paper shows that factor models should be judged by the economic exposures they represent and the portfolio decisions those exposures support, alongside return fit.

Working papers

Same Forecasts, Different Portfolios: Factor Normalization and Regularization

Working paper, October 2026 · 116 pages including the Internet Appendix

Two separately estimated IPCA specifications that differ only in how the loadings are normalised (Euclidean or Gram metric) give nearly identical forecasts but gross Sharpe ratios of 0.69 and 1.07 under the same numerical penalty on factor covariances. The paper derives the penalty transformation that keeps portfolio positions unchanged across equivalent model representations; applying it aligns the two portfolios (p. 3).

Characteristic-Space Metrics in Factor Models: Identification and Inference

SSRN preprint, September 2026

Two factor models can give every stock the same fitted return yet credit it differently to latent factors, observed factors and an intercept; the paper shows that the metric (inner product) chosen for characteristic space, a measurement convention or “ruler”, decides that split, and derives confidence intervals that carry the metric’s own estimation error.

Interpreting Estimated Pricing Errors: Evidence from Characteristic-Based Return Forecasts

Working paper, September 2026

Pairing each model’s expected-return estimate with each model’s factors on the same U.S. stocks, the paper finds that the alpha gap between equal- and value-weighted training mainly reflects different expected-return estimates, and that historical residual diagnostics add little to forecast selection (exploratory evidence).

Research in progress

Exposure or Intercept? Identifying Corporate Bond Premia with No-Arbitrage IPCA

With Paolo Zaffaroni · In preparation

Using No-Arbitrage IPCA (NA-IPCA) on public TRACE data, the paper asks whether corporate bond characteristics predict returns through factor exposures or through an intercept the factors leave unexplained, and how much of that split depends on the choice of ruler (the metric) and on the cap on how much the intercept may carry.
