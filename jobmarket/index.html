About

I received my PhD in Finance from Imperial College London in 2026. I am a Visiting Researcher at the Imperial Centre of Excellence in Quantitative Finance (opens in a new tab) and a part-time AI and machine-learning adviser to the Cost of Capital team at KPMG LLP (UK) (opens in a new tab).
I am on the 2026–27 academic job market.

My primary research fields are empirical asset pricing, machine learning and big data in finance, and financial econometrics. Working with large panels of stock characteristics and regularised machine-learning estimators, I ask which parts of a factor model’s answer (its factors, its alphas, its portfolios) reflect information in the data, and which reflect only how the model measures that information. I follow these measurement choices from identification and inference to the portfolios an investor ends up holding.

Research

Job market paper

Characteristic Libraries and Portfolio Decisions

Working paper, September 2026 · 87 pages including the Internet Appendix

  • Paper page
  • Paper (PDF)

Motivation. Asset managers routinely add characteristics to the libraries behind their return forecasts, and a better backtest after an update is easily read as new information. Yet repeating a characteristic adds no observation and leaves the attainable portfolios unchanged, and it can still change a regularised estimate after the penalty is retuned. The reason is the penalty: a ridge-type rule charges each column as the library writes it, so five exact copies of a characteristic cut its effective penalty to one-fifth, and one retuned penalty strength generally cannot undo a copy that favours a single theme.

Research question. If a tuned investment rule receives the same information written down differently, does it pick different stocks, and does that change what it earns after trading costs?

Method. On the Jensen–Kelly–Pedersen library of 153 U.S. stock characteristics in 13 themes, the paper holds data, stocks, dates and method fixed and changes only how the library is written: each theme in turn copied fivefold, or every theme rescaled by its number of characteristics (theme balancing). Four tuned ridge-type procedures make 179 monthly decisions, 2011–2025: blocked ridge; a ridge whose penalty is weighted by training Sharpe ratios (SR-weighted); Universal Portfolio Shrinkage (UPSA); and its single-ridge comparator, tuned by leave-one-out selection (LOO ridge). One allocation rule turns each estimate into named-stock targets, and a stock-by-stock trading account charges 10–100 basis points per dollar traded.

Findings. Theme balancing alone makes the positions each procedure wants to hold on the same date differ by 19–28% of average total position size (long plus short; a difference in targets, not trading), and shifts which themes it leans on. Whether returns after costs rise or fall depends on the method and the trading assumptions. For Universal Portfolio Shrinkage, balancing changes the annual mean after 25-basis-point costs by −0.19 percentage points (95% interval −0.57 to 0.19, which includes zero). Of the 52 single-theme copy contrasts at that cost, only two have simultaneous bands that exclude zero, and both are losses; the larger, copying the seasonality theme fivefold in the Sharpe-weighted ridge, is −1.30 percentage points a year. Separately, expanding monthly ridge from 39 to 153 characteristics changes normalised forecast loss by only 0.0079 percentage points (95% interval −0.040 to 0.068): the data cannot tell a gain from a loss, and the comparison does not separate new information from a change in regularisation. A simulation shows that test error can improve with no new information.

Contribution. Closest are portfolio-shrinkage studies such as Kelly et al. (2026), Ke and Pourmohammadi (2025) and Jensen et al. (2026), which compare methods on a given library; the regularisation channel relates to Kozak, Nagel and Santosh (2020). This paper instead holds the method fixed, varies only how the library is written, and follows the response into named-stock positions and returns after costs; for stock ridge, carrying the penalty over to the rewritten library restores the original problem exactly, which gives a control. It claims neither investable profits nor an optimal library, and the U.S. sample was also used in developing the design. The result is a diagnostic: before adopting a library update, check attribution, stock targets and returns after costs under the actual implementation.

Working papers

Characteristic-Space Metrics in Factor Models: Identification and Inference

SSRN preprint, September 2026

Two factor models can give every stock the same fitted return yet credit it differently to hidden factors, observed factors and an intercept. The paper shows that a measurement convention on characteristic space (the ruler) decides that split, and derives confidence intervals that carry the ruler’s own estimation error.

Interpreting Estimated Pricing Errors: Evidence from Characteristic-Based Return Forecasts

Working paper, September 2026

When one model leaves a smaller alpha, did its factors explain more, or did the expected-return estimate they must explain change? Pairing each model’s estimate with each model’s factors on the same U.S. stocks, the paper finds that the alpha gap between equal- and value-weighted training mainly reflects different estimates, and that alpha size adds little to choosing a forecast (exploratory evidence).

Characteristic Geometry and Portfolio Choice

Working paper, September 2026

Why can factor models with nearly identical forecasts hold different portfolios? Comparing IPCA with a version that differs only in the ruler, under one portfolio routine on rolling U.S. samples, the gap traces to the routine’s small, fixed factor ridge: removing only that constant nearly closes it.
